My name is EV, and I've spent this year discovering the beautiful world of dumbphones. When I've discussed my journey with friends, many of them have told me they share the sentiment of wanting to spend less of their lives on their phones. I'm making this guide to (hopefully) help someone else start their own dumbphone journey, because mine has given me back so much time, attention, and presence in my own life.

The different kinds of dumbphones

Minimalist smartphones (aka "dumb" smartphones)Basically a smartphone in dumb packaging. They usually run Android, so you can still download the few apps you truly need, but the small screen, slower speed, and sometimes a physical keyboard make everything else way less tempting. They can be super helpful for cutting down time spent on your phone and moving away from the most addictive tech, and they're a great fit if you're nervous about completely cutting yourself off from certain apps or features.Titan Pocketsquare screen + QWERTY keyboard (my first!)Jelly Stara teeny tiny 3-inch AndroidMinimal Phonee-ink screen + QWERTY keyboard
True dumbphones / feature phones (non-smartphones)There's a big range here. On one end are newer, simpler phones designed for modern life. They skip the stuff that pulls you in (no web browser, no app store, no social media) but often keep helpful tools a basic flip phone wouldn't have, like directions, music and podcasts, a decent camera, weather, or a hotspot. Those extras can make a big difference for getting around and feeling safe when you're out. Some of these (like mine) are "locked-down" feature phones: they can use data, but only for a few built-in tools like maps or email, and you can never add apps or a browser. On the other end is a literal flip phone that just calls and texts, and there's definitely a middle ground too! I started with a "dumb" smartphone and eventually moved to a feature phone / dumbphone, but honestly, either one is a great step!Light Phone IIIsuper minimal, just the essentialsSunbeam F1the flip phone I have now ♡Nokia 2780 Flipa classic, affordable flip

Why I started with the Titan Pocket

I did some research on r/dumbphones and decided the Titan Pocket would be a good fit for me initially. I was worried that switching straight to T9 texting might be too big of a jump and discourage me from the dumbphone journey altogether. I also liked that it was an older model running an older version of Android (Android 11), so it would be a little slower overall and generally just a little busted.

My main goal was to keep the things I NEEDED, but slow myself down and make all the non-essential stuff more inconvenient. Who wants to scroll YouTube Shorts on a 3-inch screen? (Me, sometimes, evidently. But it did help.) At the time, I felt like the apps I absolutely NEEDED were maps, rideshare apps, Okta Verify for work, and Spotify, plus the basic phone functions, and the Titan Pocket checked all of those boxes. It also had a physical QWERTY keyboard, which I loved because it was fun and definitely slowed me down.

The main thing I wanted to target was the screen. I'd noticed that the iPhone's screen, colors, and ease of navigation are a big part of what keeps you so hooked. It's almost so beautiful that it sucks you in. I figured a kind of janky, slower display alone would make me want to use my phone less. There are lots of similar options out there, usually called minimalist (or "dumb") smartphones: generally Android phones packaged in a "dumb" display.

I bought my Titan Pocket online, directly from the Unihertz website. If you're looking for a cheaper option, or the one you want is out of stock, people often sell used dumbphones on eBay and other resale sites too. The day I went to set it up, I went straight to the Verizon store, where they told me the phone wasn't going to be compatible with their network. I got in the car and drove DIRECTLY to T-Mobile that same day and told them I was looking to switch plans. Ironically, they told me that switching plans often gets you a bonus brand-new free phone… which I politely rejected, and showed the very kind (and sort of confused) employee my weird futuristic BlackBerry. I ended up switching plans, getting my physical SIM card, and activating it all that same day. Yay!!!

Tips & things to know for your transition

Get a physical SIM cardOne step of switching will likely be getting a physical SIM card. This depends on the phone, but both of the ones I've had used a physical SIM. Most newer iPhones use an eSIM (in the US, the iPhone 14 and later don't even have a SIM slot), so you'll have to either go to your carrier's store and get a physical SIM or order one online. Once you have it, switching between dumbphones gets way easier!
RIP blue textsFor my iPhone users, hold my hand while I tell you this: you will have to say goodbye to your blue text messages… dumbphones evidently do not run on Apple lol. I was pretty nervous about this when I first switched, but my lovely friends were very supportive overall. (You will get teased here and there. That's simply part of the price you pay for being a dumbphone legend.) You'll most likely be using a phone that runs Android or a simpler operating system like KaiOS (my phone's is called "BasicOS" loll). And the land of green texts is not as scary as it seems! At the end of the day, a text is a text: your friends can still reach you, and the people who matter won't care what color your text bubble is (which sounds kind of silly when you write it out, right?). If your new phone supports RCS, you'll still get a lot of the features you're used to from iMessage, like typing bubbles, reactions, read receipts, and higher-quality photos and videos, even when texting iPhone users. Plus, Android emojis are lowkey cuter, and some of them even do little dances. The phone I use now only does SMS/MMS, and it's still so, so doable to get by with texting and group chats. Do I miss an emoji here and there? Sure. It's not the end of the world, babe.
Turn off iMessage & RCS early (before you switch phones)If you're coming from an iPhone, turn off iMessage as early as you can before you switch (Apple even has a page to deregister your number). Same goes for RCS (the upgraded texting most Android phones use now) if you're switching to a phone that doesn't support it. Your number can stay tied to those systems for a while after you switch, sometimes up to a couple weeks, and in that window some texts (for me, only group chats) may not make it to your new phone. You may also need to restart some group chats with your friends from your new phone. This happened to me for the first few days when I switched. Bottom line: you might have a little adjustment period where you miss some texts. Don't freak out. It'll sort itself out! Just let your friends know, ask what you missed, and start up a new group chat if you need to. Bonus: you're already talking to people in person more :)

Even if your new phone supports RCS, if there's any chance you'll want a non-RCS-capable dumbphone down the line, I'd recommend keeping RCS off. More info on that below.
You *may* need a new carrierSome dumbphones only work on certain carriers, so check phone by phone before you buy. From what I've seen, T-Mobile and the smaller carriers that run on its network, like Mint Mobile, tend to support the most of these phones. Carriers on AT&T's network, like Cricket, often work too. Verizon can be pickier about which phones it allows, though some phones do work on it. Most dumbphone makers post a carrier compatibility list, so look for that first.
InconvenienceBe prepared for things to take longer or be a little harder. Texting takes a bit longer, you may need to carry your physical cards around again, and you might not be able to look up who invented the jelly bean or Venmo your friend right on the spot. This is OK!!! Honestly, our urgency to do those things right away shows how often we step out of real life to do something that could wait. Sometimes it's okay to just wonder :) More on that below! You'll also start to realize how much of the world is built for a smartphone: QR code menus at restaurants, rideshare apps, mobile-only concert tickets and boarding passes, and the list goes on. Don't fret! Lucky for you, this guide covers exactly how to deal with all that!

Life with my weird little BlackBerry

I didn't really prepare for my transition ahead of time. It was sort of a fed-up, last-straw decision that I didn't really consult anyone about. I kind of just came out of the portal of a random February weekend with my weird little futuristic BlackBerry and, like, half of my hair cut off (a hair chop is not required for dumbphone life, but bonus points for some extra chaos to keep things spicy). The reactions were certainly mixed but overwhelmingly POSITIVE, especially from people our age, which I didn't quite expect. I think a lot of people are quietly holding onto a lot of mixed feelings about smartphones, and hey, I get why.

Immediately, I noticed a huge difference in how much I would get sucked into my phone. It's objectively much harder when your experience is annoying and your screen isn't great to look at. Trying to navigate apps that aren't meant for a 3-inch square screen means things are cut off, you can't click certain things, and everything is tiny, squished, and inconvenient. (I liked to imagine the probably singular guy at every company who was hired to program their software to work on a Titan Pocket.) This alone did wonders in getting me off of most apps and shifting me over to doing these things on my computer instead. Some of these included checking the weather, any banking apps, Venmo for the most part, shopping/Depop/DoorDash, YouTube, streaming apps, and tbh even just browsing the web in general (social media not mentioned since I had already gotten off virtually all of those apps before my switch).

I also noticed just how often I reached for my phone, because that habit didn't fade right away. It was humbling (and a whole new experience) to grab the device, realize there finally wasn't a constant stream of notifications and content trying to steal my attention, and then have no option but to… put it down? That cycle definitely repeated itself until my brain learned there just wasn't much interesting on there anymore.

Any time I got lots of texts in the middle of the workday or when I was busy, I would be deterred by the effort it would take (either typing on the physical keyboard or having to speak out loud), and this would naturally result in me just waiting until I got home or had downtime, which I loved. The slowness of doing anything on the phone gave me extra time to think twice about whether something really needed to be done on my phone right then, and it let me choose to stay present far more often. My screen time dropped from a previously heinously high number on my iPhone to more like an hour or two a day, which was mostly texting or Spotify/YouTube, and I definitely started to rely on it and check it less.

Eventually, however, I started to indulge in what was actually left on the phone. I found myself still scrolling YouTube Shorts (which is frankly insane, because the videos are about an inch wide), going through stale Strava posts I'd already seen that day, and constantly relying on the web browser to answer my every thought or question throughout the day. It also started to increasingly bother me that even with my "dumb"-looking device, I still felt inundated with random internet news I'd see just from opening Google, or with notifications from apps and other addictive features designed to pull me back in. In a way, it felt like the pull of the phone and its apps had found its way back to me, even on my new setup, and I just wanted to be away from it for good.

Shortly after, I found myself going down a bit of a phone-addiction YouTube rabbit hole, which led me to lots of firsthand accounts from awesome people who had switched to some form of true dumbphone or feature phone and documented their experience: how they did things, what life looked like, and so on. No apps, no browser, no endless scroll. (Many of the videos that inspired me are linked up in the research section.) I was very intrigued and decided I wanted to give it a try, and that's when I landed on the Sunbeam!! I went in knowing that a flip phone with no apps and no browser was going to be a bit of a jump, so I decided to treat it as an experiment, with no pressure to fully commit and the option to go back to my Titan Pocket if it was too much. Now, I can't really imagine going back.

Life with my silly little flip phone

I was SO excited for this phone to come, and not just because I would get to hang up on people by snapping my flip phone shut on them (although that was a big part of it). I'd never really known what it felt like to not have the whole internet in my pocket, and I really wanted to find out. When it finally came, I flipped it open to see a list of tools: messages, contacts, calls and voicemail, email, calculator, clock, and so on. The list runs out quite quickly. It does have a few genuinely cool extras, though, like a weather app (honestly better than the Titan Pocket's), navigation, a music player, a flashlight (concert approved), and a hotspot I can use in a pinch. I tested out the T9 texting, played around with the calculator (turns out you can calculate a loan payment on this thing?!), took a few potato-quality photos, and that was it. No apps, no browser, no constant stimulation.

Using a phone this limited has made me realize just how many everyday tools have quietly been folded into our smartphones over the years. Your phone is your wallet, your map, your camera, your alarm clock, your concert ticket, your music player, your ride home… of course it feels impossible to not have it in your hand every 5 minutes. I felt tied to and deeply reliant on something that wasn't making me feel good. Now that my phone can do so little, I've had to find new ways to do all of those things, and it's actually been really fun :)

How do I do X on this phone?

The questions everyone asks me, and how I handled each one on both of my phones.

What's texting like?
Titan Pocket

The biggest shift here was the physical keyboard, but I appreciated how much it slowed me down with my texting. I previously had a tendency to feel like I needed to respond immediately, and to overexplain myself and text excessively. I found that the physical keyboard slowing me down gave me more time to think through my responses, even as I was typing them, and not overexplain myself so much. Since it was an Android phone, it used Google Messages, which you can pair with your computer online through Google Messages for web. A lot of the time, this resulted in me actually waiting until I got home to respond to messages on my computer, which I really liked because it meant I wasn't constantly texting all day. One downside was that since my messages were always on my computer, they would sometimes distract me there. I could also send voice notes and do voice-to-text, which came in super handy! I used RCS messaging on this phone, and it worked great: it lets you send higher-quality photos and videos and see typing bubbles and reactions, even with iPhone users. If you think you might want a non-RCS-capable dumbphone someday, though, see the note below.

Sunbeam

The T9 predictive engine on this phone is actually a lot better than you might think, but don't get me wrong, it's slow. Sunbeam offers an additional feature, which I purchased, that allows me to do voice-to-text, and the phone can actually send voice memos as well. I use both of these most of the time. Since it's technically a touchscreen as well, I'll sometimes type out a word here or there with the extremely tiny little flip phone touchscreen letters. The Sunbeam uses SMS and MMS for texting, which means no reactions (they come through as their own text message, like Loved "dumbphones rule!!!"), and no typing bubbles or read receipts (which I personally don't find necessary). This phone specifically also can't receive videos, but that isn't the case for all dumbphones. When someone wants to send me one, I just have them iMessage it to my email address, where I can watch it on my computer, or send it to my actual email. Or show me in person :) The Sunbeam also has an option to add a text signature, which I would HIGHLY recommend because it makes you sound like a dad who just learned how to text (which I deeply enjoy. Make your phone fun and silly again!!). Overall, again, this just encourages me to not spend my entire day derailing what I'm doing to immediately respond to everyone, but if I'm in a pinch, I can get a text out quickly. I definitely call people a lot more often now, which I love :)- EV

For either phone: Both the Titan Pocket and the Sunbeam only run on 4G, which has been totally fine for texting and calls in my experience. I've even randomly had data in spots where my friends haven't!

What about group chats?
Titan Pocket

Group chats are fully supported on this phone through RCS. Like I mentioned in the tips above, after switching from iPhone to Android, you may need to restart some of your group chats that used to be all iMessage. When people texted me in those old chats, I just wouldn't get the messages anywhere (not on my phone or my computer). Just give your friends a heads-up that any old blue iMessage threads with you won't reach your new phone, since it doesn't support iMessage. Once we restarted the chats, I never had that issue again. Other than that, I had very minimal issues here. I used RCS messaging on this phone, and it made group chats work smoothly: photos, videos, and reactions all came through just like they would in iMessage. But if there's any chance you'll want a non-RCS-capable dumbphone someday, my most important piece of advice is to turn off RCS messaging as early as possible, or to never enable it in the first place (on phones that use Google Messages, you can opt out). More on that below.

Sunbeam

SMS/MMS group chats are fully supported on the Sunbeam, and they've worked great for me. I still plan plenty of hangouts on my dumbphone! For bigger group events, I usually use Partiful, and for smaller plans, I just start a group chat or text people 1:1 (and if a group chat ever acts up, the fixes below have me covered). Same as above: I can receive photos but not videos (see above for my workaround!). The one place I did run into trouble was my transition off the RCS cloud, which caused me to miss some group messages. The good news is that group texts should eventually go back to normal, but unfortunately it's not automatic right away. I break down everything I went through below: the timeline, the issues I ran into, and how I fixed them.

must-know info about navigating group chats on a dumbphone (no RCS)

One of the trickier issues people transitioning to a dumbphone run into, based on my own experience and some other accounts on the r/dumbphones subreddit, is detransitioning from RCS messaging and how that affects group chats. Unfortunately, this is not an issue with any dumbphones themselves, but rather with Apple's and Google's systems, which do not make it clear or easy for other phones to recognize that our phones cannot receive their advanced messaging features for some time after we've switched away from them. To me, this feels like another one of the ways these companies make us reliant on their products and systems for everyday communication. Luckily, people in the dumbphone community, including myself, have been testing out lots of ways to get around this and push back against these systems. That said, everyone's experience with RCS is different, and some people switch to a dumbphone without running into any issues at all. Here's what happened for me:

  1. I transitioned from the Titan Pocket, which supports RCS, to the Sunbeam F1 Horizon, which does not. Before I switched phones, I turned RCS off on my old phone AND deregistered my number from RCS through Google's deregistration website. This is a vital step to get off of RCS. I had a tiny bit of trouble receiving select messages during the first day or two of the transition (which I think was mostly from moving my SIM card to a new phone), but overall it resolved itself very quickly, including in existing group chats. For about a month, I had no issues at all.
  2. Google's RCS system removes you from all RCS group chats after 30 days, and this is where I started to run into some issues. Right around the one-month mark on my new phone, I stopped receiving certain group messages because those threads were stuck in RCS. Individual texts were still working totally fine. Stale threads after switching messaging protocols are unfortunately a known issue, so I expected to have to restart some text threads. However, I noticed that even new threads were attempting to send my phone RCS group messages, which it can't receive. Here's how I fixed it (for the most part), ranked by how much each fix helped me:
    • What's worked best for me: For any group chat where you aren't receiving messages, it helps to add a member that forces the chat into SMS/MMS. There's a range of fake, non-working phone numbers that are reserved for movies and TV and aren't assigned to anyone: 555-0100 through 555-0199, which you can use with your area code (for example, (your area code) 555-0123). I added one of these numbers to the chats that were stuck for me (I affectionately named mine "RCS Busta" in my contacts) and immediately started receiving all the messages from people in them. In my opinion, this is the most frictionless option, since it doesn't require anyone to change their phone settings or delete their old text history. (Just make sure to stick to that 0100–0199 range, since other 555 numbers aren't guaranteed to be fake.)
    • A close second: I asked some of my close friends who are in a lot of my group chats to turn RCS off on their phones. Having at least one SMS warrior (aka a friend with RCS off on their phone) in my group chats send messages from an iPhone seemed to shift the thread to SMS/MMS and sort of freed me from "RCS jail" for those threads. This worked on a thread-by-thread basis, and it did the trick for most of my group chats. One downside: if those friends are also in group chats with Android users, turning RCS off could cause them to miss texts from Android users that are sent over RCS, so it's not totally ideal. For friends who'd rather keep RCS on, you can ask them to keep an eye out for any group chat you're in that shows an "RCS" label, and to give you a heads-up or text you 1:1 instead.
    • Only one of my group chats wouldn't seem to "stick" in SMS mode: one with a mix of iPhone and Android users (plus me). For that one, the Android user and I switched to using our computers, and I started a new group chat through iMessage using my email. While it's not 100% ideal, it's a good workaround or temporary fix if you're running into issues. This could also work with a third-party app like WhatsApp or Signal. And while you're sorting things out, be sure to let people know to text you anything important 1:1!
    • Another Sunbeam user I came across shared a fix that worked for them: have your friends delete any old RCS threads with you in them (group chats AND 1-on-1s, even ones from months ago), or at least remove you from them. Then text each friend individually with a regular SMS so their phone "learns" you're an SMS number, and finally start a brand-new group chat yourself. In my experience, though, it's hard to get everyone to delete every chat they've ever had with you (people understandably want to keep their text history!), so asking a few people to turn off RCS was the easier fix for me.
    • I also tried working with T-Mobile to get the "Advanced Messaging" flag on my line shut off, so other phones wouldn't see my number as RCS-capable when they send me a message. After a lot of (deep, slightly insane) Reddit research and digging through online phone forums, I found this advice from a fellow T-Mobile user. Unfortunately, I wasn't able to get through to them properly, and after many calls back and forth, they told me this would be something that has to be fixed on my end. So while it worked for this user, I didn't have any luck here, but maybe you will!
      The phone carrier fix (explanation + script to read them)

      Why it happens: Even after you switch phones, your number can stay flagged as "RCS-capable" in your carrier's system. Deregistering from iMessage doesn't touch this, since it's a completely separate system. When your friends' iPhones check whether you can receive RCS, the carrier says yes, so their messages get sent as RCS, can't be delivered to your dumbphone, and just silently disappear.

      Step 1: Call T-Mobile. Ask for "Advanced Messaging" to be deprovisioned at the account level on your line. Front-line reps usually don't know how, so ask to escalate to Tier 2 and have them open a back-end engineering ticket. For this user, it took 2–3 days. Here's the script they used:

      "My line was previously on an iPhone and is now on a [your phone] with no RCS capability. The number is still flagged as RCS-capable in your registry, causing iPhone senders to route RCS messages that can't be delivered. I need the Advanced Messaging entitlement removed at the account level. This isn't a device setting. Please open a back-end ticket with the provisioning team."

      My tip: If you give this a try, I'd recommend giving them all the info about your past and current phones. Tell them clearly that you've already deregistered from RCS on your end, and have them read their notes back to you to make sure everything sounds right before they escalate it to engineering.

      Another fix this user suggested for group chats: An existing group thread stays stuck on whatever it started as, and starting a "new" chat with the exact same people just drops you back into the old one. The trick is to change who's in the group: add (or remove) one person so phones treat it as a brand-new thread, which then starts out as regular MMS.

      Source: a T-Mobile user's write-up on r/LightPhone. This is advice from Reddit, not T-Mobile, so results may vary!

Based on other experiences I've seen online, I'm hoping this issue will keep resolving itself with time. The longer it's been since you switched, the more time these systems have to fully deregister your number, and the more chances your friends' phones have to refresh what they've saved about your number and stop treating it as RCS-capable. I'm planning to give it some more time and see how it goes!

At the end of the day, I realized missing some group messages is not the end of the world for me. While group texting is a capability I'd count as essential in a phone (and I wouldn't keep using this exact phone if I hadn't been able to quickly resolve probably 99% of these issues with the fixes above), during the couple of days I spent fixing it, it honestly wasn't so bad. I ended up calling friends that week, texting people 1:1 more, and some of my friends and I even started email threads, which have been so fun and silly. Something beautiful about this process is that even the bumps and inconveniences along the way usually lead to something sweet. Along these lines, I realized that even group texts can sometimes be one of the ways we're distant or impersonal with each other. My dad and I ended up having a 30-minute talk over the phone that started with discussing the texting issue, and without it, we would've just continued to occasionally respond to each other in our family group chat. While I still deem group chats an essential feature for myself, it was just another eye-opening moment :,)

Picking up takeout in person means saying hi to the owner. Buying a CD from a record store means getting recs from the shop guy and getting to spend a road trip taking turns switching out CDs, feeling the joy and wonder of music you now physically own.

The world is increasingly built around a device that isn't really bringing us much happiness, one we now physically rely on and keep within arm's reach at basically all times, to the point where simply not using one takes all the workarounds I just walked you through. Honestly, I just don't think we were built to get closer, day by day, to being the people in WALL-E, never going anywhere or talking to anyone. People are lonelier than ever, more isolated than ever, and sadder than ever. Despite everything being so… convenient? And while this ultra-convenience culture takes away all the little annoyances, it also takes away the little joys :,(

A little disclaimer ♡

This guide is not here to force you or beg you to get rid of your smartphone. I truly believe that has to come from you wanting it yourself, not from someone else telling you to. It's just here to hopefully shed some light on the process and help out anyone who's been feeling this way and hoping for a solution. And this is just what has worked for ME. Everyone is different, and some people are super capable of setting limits on their smartphone that work great for them. Unfortunately, the addictive tech that got me was a lot better at its job than I was at resisting it, so I needed a more drastic measure.

Not ready to switch? Some things to try first:

  • Make your phone harder to use: Brick is a little physical device that blocks your distracting apps until you tap your phone on it again, and Foqos is an app that does the same thing with NFC tags you can stick anywhere or with QR codes.
  • Built-in tricks: there are tons of these, like app limits, app blockers, and turning your screen black and white. These never quite stuck for me, which is why I wanted to see if something else would, but they work for a lot of people!

My story

I think we can all relate to the feeling of kind of hating our phones a little bit? Whether it's the hours spent scrolling, the content that floods in the moment you open your phone in the morning, or the fact that it feels virtually impossible to exist without it – phones just have a hold on us. We rarely even go from one room to the next without them. This feeling had been quietly creeping in for me over the past few years, and it was slowly growing harder to ignore. Looking back at the 2026 ins and outs list I made with my friends, it's quite apparent where my head was at.

The list is funny, but it's also a real indication of the frustration that I had built up around smartphones and navigating modern relationships online. Last year, I bought a book about mindfulness because I genuinely felt like my head was all over the place, and I was trying to figure out how to slow my thoughts down a little. The more I read about the author's experience with mindfulness, the more I noticed how rarely I was truly present for most things. His writing explores how ordinary tasks, like washing the dishes, can actually be a chance to practice being present. Rather than rushing through them to get to the next thing, he talks about letting your attention rest entirely on the dishes in front of you, taking in each step of the process. The same goes for walking down the street: paying attention to the walk itself and whatever you happen to come across along the way, instead of rushing to get somewhere while distracted or preoccupied. Upon reading this book, I realized that one of the very few times I felt fully present was when I was biking – a hobby that had coincidentally become one of my absolute favorite things to do in the year or two before. Biking is almost a practice of mindfulness in itself: hands on the handlebars, phone tucked away, eyes on the road, with no option but to take in everything in front of you.

The flip side of that realization was noticing just how often I wasn't being present in my day-to-day life, and how much modern phone culture was keeping me from it. I'd wash the dishes while watching a show on my phone. I'd walk down the street with music blasting in my ears, or honestly, sometimes texting or looking something up as I walked. At concerts, I'd catch myself filming for later instead of just watching with my own eyes, spending half the show waiting for the perfect moment to capture. And I don't think I'm alone in that. Phones beg for our attention in a way that makes it hard to go even 10 minutes without being pulled out of the moment.

Some days, I was so distracted and overstimulated that by the time I felt like I could finally focus on anything, it was already 1 or 2 PM. I always found it jarring how quickly the mornings vanished. It legit felt like I'd been pulled into the Lotus Casino from Percy Jackson, like I was in a trance, and by the time I finally snapped out of it, hours had gone by without me. I'd start thinking about weekend plans or a chore I needed to do, and then I'd get a text. That would lead to opening more texts, and then to checking Instagram. And Depop. And Strava. I would have a million trains of thought that would never quite make it to the station.

And honestly, that behavior wasn't entirely my fault – smartphones are designed to draw us in that way. The documentary The Social Dilemma challenges us to ask ourselves why social media and content platforms are free in the first place. The haunting truth is that the apps were never the product: we are, and so is our time. We're paying for them with hours of our lives, and that really did not sit right with me.

It wasn't just the time I was wasting on these apps that bothered me – it was also what I was consuming. I think we've all noticed short-form videos chipping away at our attention spans, but beyond the format, the content itself was starting to get to me. The stuff that does well is the stuff that makes you want to click: something shocking, scary, or sad, anything that makes you feel big emotions. Being fed a random stream of highs and lows was giving me constant emotional whiplash, and it wasn't doing my mental health any good – if anything, quite the opposite. I noticed the same thing in news headlines, which only seemed to get more sensational and alarmist. It all kept me in this constant state of doom, looking for something to numb it, and picking up my phone to do that once again.

Being on social media also made it harder to think for myself. I was constantly seeing this groupthink rhetoric of "we hate this," "this is cancelled," and so on, and with so many people telling me what to think, it got harder to even know what I actually thought. I felt like I had to go along with my corner of the internet on pretty much everything. But sometimes there were takes that frankly didn't make sense to me, and instead of simply thinking, "Hey, I don't agree with that," I could feel myself trying to fit INTO the take, almost questioning my own opinion and wondering what I must be missing, since supposedly everyone in my online circles agreed. Looking back, that's kind of scary. This groupthink mentality can make people feel like they can't have even a slightly different opinion, and I think that's really damaging. I'd so much rather be having conversations and disagreeing than shutting people out at the scent of a slightly differing opinion. It's pretty ironic how anti-social social media has made us.

Beyond my own focus and mental health, there was another issue I slowly identified over the years: how my phone was shaping my relationships. As a somewhat anxious person, I've always found that keeping up with people online does me more harm than good. I think most of us have felt this with Instagram or other social media apps: everyone is posting a highly curated snapshot of their life, and it's easy to forget that it's just a snapshot and start comparing your real life to it. On top of that, I'd catch myself trying to glean all kinds of social information from it: who was commenting on whose posts, who seemed to be friends with who, the FOMO over a reality that didn't even really exist. Location sharing gives me that same uneasy feeling. It's great for safety purposes, but to me it's very odd how normal it's become to need to know exactly where everyone in our lives is at all times, or what they're doing. It all made me feel like a piece of my brain was dedicated to keeping tabs on everyone, and I couldn't shut it down. Our brains just aren't meant to know all of this, all the time.

Then there was the other side of it: keeping up my own presence online. After posting, I'd feel anxious checking who had seen it, who commented, and how it was "doing." Excessive texting came with its own anxiety: the miscommunications, the unclear tone, the waiting when someone wasn't responding. Living so much of my social life online even changed how I saw people. Sometimes I'd find someone's profile before really knowing them, and their posts, or even the way they texted, would intimidate me or make me judge them before we'd ever really hung out, even when they turned out to be completely different in person. I'd rather know people as who they are when I'm with them.

I realized it was almost like living in a parallel universe. I had to maintain a digital version of myself to interact with the digital versions of my friends, who I was somehow now seeking approval from? That felt so unnatural to me. I never feel that anxiety when I share my life with my friends in person. And the more time we spend keeping up that parallel universe, the more it isolates us from this one. You can see it anywhere you go: sitting on the bus surrounded by phone zombies can feel kind of eerie and lonely when everyone around you is staring at a screen, tapped into their digital universe instead of looking up at their real one. It really made me wonder: are our phones actually connecting us less?

I tried deleting apps to help, starting with TikTok a couple years back. When it wasn't TikTok, it was Instagram, so I deleted Instagram after that. By 2025, I'd gotten rid of virtually every other social media app I had left. And in 2026, it was still Depop, my PHOTOS APP. Literally. ANYTHING. Anything to avoid sitting with something that felt even a little challenging, or to combat the smallest bit of boredom. In its own way, my phone was a puff of a vape for thinking. Any time I felt overwhelmed or something felt hard, I'd instinctively reach for it to replace that feeling with some form of numbing content. It felt like time to start recognizing that as the addiction it was.

I had been joking for years about hoping I would lose my phone, that I would get a dumbphone, that one day I'd just microwave it. But it never felt like a real option. No one around me had one. Even people with Androids seemed to get left out, so a flip phone felt out of the question. But I couldn't shake the feeling that something needed to change. A phone addiction might not seem outwardly as debilitating as other addictions, but when I thought about how often I was spending my time staring at a tiny box instead of going out to enjoy the day or spending time with people, over and over again, I felt like there had to be something I could do. So finally, one Sunday morning, after finding myself in a phone hole instead of out enjoying my life, I decided to just… look into it a little bit. And it turns out there were tons of people who had made the switch, and so many others who felt the same way I did. That's when I started to learn everything that I'll now share in my guide :)

I've been off most social media for two years and have been using a dumbphone for about 8 months now, and I can genuinely say the not knowing has been awesome for me. I don't think my brain was meant to know as much as it did all the time. In a good way, my world feels a little smaller now, and it's made life feel so much less overwhelming, like my brain can actually handle the amount of social interaction and stimulation that it gets. I hear updates from my friends when I see them. We show each other photos over dinner, at a get-together, or during an intentional phone call. The two things I hear most are, "But I'd miss my sister's Instagram stories!" and "Don't you feel out of the loop?" And I get it. There are positive things about smartphones and social media, and they're not ALL bad. But for me personally, the negatives outweigh the positives. I'd rather call my family, have them text or even email me pictures, and hear all the details of a story instead of just passively knowing that my friend went on a trip to Europe without having talked to them for the past 8 months. I call people way more than I used to, and I'm able to stay pretty connected through texting too. You do lose the ease of connecting with everyone all the time, but for me it's really deepened how much I invest in my community in real life. I spend a lot of time making plans, gathering people, and calling my long-distance friends and family, which is the kind of effort deep relationships need to last. And if anything, it's given me more time and focus for the important people in my life, and I feel like I have a true community more than ever.

My phone has gone from something I actively disliked to something full of the things I love: messages and photos from my family and friends, and tools to help me get through my day. It finally does what a phone is supposed to do. And I can feel the difference in myself, too. I'm much more comfortable with boredom, I generally feel less anxious, and I have more fun and creative ideas (like making this guide!). Time-wasting stuff doesn't pull me in like it used to, because I think my brain can actually see it for what it is now. And most of what I know about the people in my life comes from actually spending time with them, which I love. If any of this resonates with you, I really hope you give it a try :)

I once saw a YouTube comment that said the internet used to just be a place in our homes, not a constant part of our lives. That really stuck with me. And no one is going to be on their deathbed wishing they'd spent more time on their phone. If anything, it'll probably be the opposite.
